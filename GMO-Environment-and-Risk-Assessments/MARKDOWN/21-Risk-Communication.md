## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish **risk communication** from hazard communication and from advocacy.
2. Communicate probabilities, comparisons, and uncertainty without sensationalism or false reassurance.
3. Diagnose poor communication (both alarmist and complacent) and repair it.
4. Plan two-way stakeholder communication: receiving knowledge, not just transmitting conclusions.
5. Understand trust: what builds it, what destroys it, why it is slow to build and fast to fall.

---

## Definition

**Risk communication** is the interactive exchange of information about risk among assessors, decision-makers, stakeholders, and the public — covering the nature of the risk, its magnitude and uncertainty, management options, and the reasoning behind decisions. It is a *process*, not a press release.

## Why it matters

Technically perfect assessments fail socially when communicated as verdicts ("safe," "dangerous"), when uncertainty is hidden (later revealed = trust collapse), or when stakeholders discover their knowledge was never solicited. Most GMO controversies worldwide are at least partly communication failures layered on genuine value disagreements — and scientists who cannot communicate uncertainty honestly become unreliable narrators in public debate.

## Beginner explanation

Three ways to say the same wrong thing:
- Alarmist: "GM pollen kills butterflies!" (a lab hazard stated as a field risk).
- Complacent: "Completely safe, proven." (no assessment says that).
- Honest: "For species X, estimated exposure is about 100× below the dose causing effects in laboratory studies; confidence is moderate; we monitor for Y each year."

The third sentence is the skill this module teaches.

## Scientific explanation

### 21.1 Hazard vs risk in communication

| Communicating... | Sounds like | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| Hazard only | "It produces an insecticidal protein" | omits exposure → fear without context |
| Risk properly | "...at exposures ~100× below effect levels for tested non-targets, monitored annually" | requires care, earns trust |

Public communication almost always fails by sliding into hazard-only language — in *both* directions ("GMOs are toxins" / "no hazard found anywhere, ever"). Discipline: every claim carries its exposure and uncertainty clauses.

### 21.2 Communicating uncertainty

Principles (evidence-informed):

1. **Levels, not walls:** qualitative bands ("low/moderate/high confidence") are understood better than naked percentages by general audiences.
2. **Range + best estimate:** "somewhere between X and Y, most likely near Z."
3. **Uncertainty ≠ ignorance:** say what would reduce it ("a two-year field study would narrow this range").
4. **No weaponized uncertainty:** "we can't be 100% sure it's harmful" is true of *everything* and is not a risk argument; conversely "no effect found" must carry its detection limits (Module 16 §16.3).
5. **Consistency:** the same statement rules in the report, the press briefing, and the farmer meeting — trust dies on discovered double-speak.

### 21.3 Communicating probability and comparison

- **Frequency beats probability:** "1 in 5,000 seeds" beats "0.02%"; natural frequencies are the best-understood format.
- **Relevant comparisons only:** compare with the alternative practice (what happens without the GMO?), not with zero risk (nothing has zero risk).
- **Absolute before relative:** "risk doubled" (relative) without the base rate (absolute) is the classic misleading frame.
- **Avoid false precision:** "37.4% less risk" implies measurement quality that rarely exists; "roughly one-third less, with a range of a quarter to a half" is more honest and more useful.

### 21.4 The two-way obligation

Risk communication is dialogue: local ecological knowledge (where do the wild relatives grow? when do larvae feed?), farmer practice realities (will refuges actually be planted?), and community values (which risks matter here?) all *belong in problem formulation* (Module 3 §3.1). Mechanisms: stakeholder workshops pre-assessment, public comment windows on dossiers, farmer-facing monitoring partnerships, plain-language assessment summaries.

### 21.5 Trust: the operating asset

| Builds trust | Destroys trust |
|---|---|
| candor about what isn't known | discovered certainty that was false |
| admitting and correcting errors | never admitting anything |
| consistent statements across audiences | different numbers per audience |
| visible listening (input changes the plan) | consultation as ritual |
| pre-committed triggers, kept | thresholds quietly moved |
| independent review | only self-review |

### 21.6 Repairing bad communication (worked examples)

**Example 1 (alarmist headline):** "Study shows GM crop toxin harms beneficial insect."
*Repair:* "In laboratory feeding at doses well above field exposure, larvae of species X showed reduced survival. Field-exposure studies found exposures far below effect levels; monitoring continues. The finding justifies tier-2 study design, not field risk conclusions."
*(Each clause does specific work: dose context, exposure reality, what the finding is for.)*

**Example 2 (complacent brief):** "GMO is completely safe for the environment."
*Repair:* "Assessment found no identified pathway with more than low risk under the approved management plan; confidence is moderate-to-high for tested endpoints; residual uncertainties (long-term resistance dynamics, untested species) are addressed by the monitoring program with defined triggers."
*(Same conclusion class, now falsifiable and trust-bearing.)*

**Example 3 (false reassurance on uncertainty):** "There is no evidence of harm."
*Repair:* "Studies to date, designed to detect effects of size X and larger, found none; smaller or longer-term effects cannot be excluded by these designs, which is why monitoring measures [indicator]."
*(Absence of evidence gets its proper sentence.)*

## Step-by-step workflow: building a communication plan

```text
1. Inventory audiences (regulator, farmers, community, media, critics)
2. Per audience: what do they need to decide/act? what do they fear/misunderstand?
3. Core messages: per pathway — hazard, exposure, conclusion, uncertainty (one paragraph each)
4. Format per audience: technical annex ↔ plain summary ↔ field card
5. Two-way channels: how input enters problem formulation & monitoring
6. Error protocol: how corrections get issued (pre-announce this!)
7. Consistency lock: same numbers everywhere; change control on figures
```

## Common misconceptions

| Misconception | Reality |
|---|---|
| "If people understood the science, they'd agree" | Values and trust legitimately enter; agreement is not the goal — informed judgment is |
| "Uncertainty scares people" | Hidden uncertainty discovered later scares them more; *explained* uncertainty builds trust |
| "Communication is one-way expertise delivery" | Dialogue is required by frameworks (Cartagena public-awareness provisions; national regimes) and by effectiveness |
| "Critic = enemy" | Organized critics have repeatedly surfaced real issues (resistance compliance, exposure gaps) — use them |
