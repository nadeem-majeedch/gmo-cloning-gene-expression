# Module 23 — Case Studies

**Level:** Intermediate → Advanced

---

## How to use this module

Each case follows one template: **Background → GMO/trait → Purpose → Potential hazards → Exposure pathways → Evidence → Risk assessment → Uncertainty → Risk management → Monitoring → Scientific conclusion → Discussion questions.** Cases are teaching constructs built around well-documented GMO categories; numbers shown as *illustrative* are simulated for classroom use, and students should consult primary literature and regulator dossiers for real figures.

The cases deliberately span outcomes — approvals, resistance evolution, controversy — because risk assessment is not a story where the "right answer" is always "no risk."

---

## Case 1 — Bt cotton

- **Background:** Cotton suffers major losses from bollworms (*Helicoverpa armigera* complex, *Pectinophora gossypiella*). Bt cotton expresses Cry proteins (e.g., Cry1Ac) toxic to certain lepidopteran larvae after midgut activation.
- **Purpose:** Reduce insecticide spraying and yield loss.
- **Potential hazards:** Non-target effects on beneficial insects; resistance evolution in target pests; gene flow to wild relatives (limited where no compatible wild *Gossypium* occurs, as in most Bt-cotton regions).
- **Exposure pathways:** Larvae feeding on Bt plant tissue; pollen; soil residues from crop debris; predators consuming intoxicated larvae.
- **Evidence:** Multi-season field comparisons of spray regimes and natural-enemy counts; resistance monitoring (bioassays of field populations); SE Asian and Chinese farm-level studies.
- **Risk assessment:** Direct non-target hazard considered low relative to the broad-spectrum insecticide regime Bt replaced — a comparative point often missed. Resistance is the dominant quantified risk.
- **Uncertainty:** Regional variation in refuge compliance; baseline natural-enemy variability.
- **Risk management:** Structured refuges where enforced; IPM integration; stewardship programmes.
- **Monitoring:** Annual susceptibility bioassays; reports of unexpected damage.
- **Conclusion:** Environmental risk profile dominated by resistance evolution, manageable with effective refuges — the binding constraint is grower compliance, not laboratory science.
- **Discussion:** Why is resistance risk higher where refuges are voluntary? What comparator should a non-target study use?

## Case 2 — Bt maize and monarch butterflies (a classic of exposure science)

- **Background:** After a 1999 laboratory note reported monarch larvae harmed by Bt-maize pollen on milkweed, the question became: is field *exposure* high enough to matter?
- **Exposure pathways:** Pollen drift onto milkweed at field margins; larval feeding window vs anthesis timing.
- **Evidence:** A multi-group US collaboration quantified pollen densities across the landscape, toxicity per pollen type (Cry1Ab event MON810/Bt11 vs Cry9C vs Cry1F), and overlap between anthesis and larval presence. Conclusion: the risk from currently grown events was negligible at landscape scale — largely because *exposure* is far lower than the laboratory dose.
- **Lesson:** Laboratory hazard without field exposure is not risk; a hazard finding is the start, not the end, of assessment. Also a lesson in communication: a single lab result became a global headline before exposure analysis existed.
- **Discussion:** Which measured variable most reduced the risk estimate? How should scientists communicate a hazard finding that later exposure data deflates?

## Case 3 — Herbicide-tolerant crops and weed-shift dynamics

- **Background:** HT crops tolerate glyphosate (or glufosinate), simplifying weed control.
- **Hazards:** Not the tolerance trait per se, but (i) volunteer HT plants carrying the trait and (ii) weed-shift/resistance dynamics in the *management system* — repeated glyphosate use selecting resistant weeds (see Case 6).
- **Exposure:** Seed spillage and harvest losses → volunteers; trait flow to related crops (e.g., oilseed rape volunteers persisting years in seed banks).
- **Assessment outcome:** Trait-volunteers are usually manageable with common tillage/herbicide rotation; the system-level herbicide-use change is the larger ecological lever.
- **Discussion:** Why is "herbicide-tolerant crops create superweeds" a misleading shorthand? What is the accurate statement about resistance?

## Case 4 — Gene flow from GM oilseed rape

- **Background:** *Brassica napus* outcrosses, has relatives in the *Brassica* complex, and forms a persistent seed bank.
- **Evidence base:** Farm-scale studies and reviews quantify gene flow via pollen (mostly short-range, tail events at distance) and seed dispersal (machinery, spillage).
- **Assessment:** Frequency of introgression into wild relatives under European conditions is generally very low and fitness consequences judged small; nevertheless gene-stacking in volunteers is a real management issue (tolerance to multiple herbicides narrowing control options).
- **Management:** Separation distances, sowing-date rules, volunteer-control guidance, harvest-machinery hygiene.
- **Discussion:** Why does a very low gene-flow frequency not automatically mean negligible risk? (Fitness and management consequences determine impact.)

## Case 5 — StarLink maize (a regulatory failure case)

- **Background:** A Cry9C maize approved (2000) only for **feed** use in the US — not food — because of unresolved allergenicity concerns (slower digestibility). It entered the human food supply via commingling, forcing recalls and export disruption.
- **Lessons:** (i) Segregation of approved-vs-unapproved uses is a real exposure-management problem; (ii) allergenicity assessment drives real decisions; (iii) monitoring and traceability are not paperwork luxuries.
- **Discussion:** Which assessment failure was scientific, and which was logistical? What single control would have prevented the episode?

## Case 6 — Glyphosate-resistant weeds

- **Background:** Intensive glyphosate reliance in HT systems selected resistant weed populations (e.g., *Conyza*, *Amaranthus*, *Lolium* spp.) across the Americas.
- **Assessment frame:** This is **resistance evolution under selection pressure** (Module 11–12 logic applied to weeds rather than insects) — a risk of the *management system*, materialised.
- **Management responses:** Herbicide mixtures/rotations, integrated weed management, mechanical control; stacked-trait HT systems emerged in response.
- **Discussion:** Was the hazard the GM trait or the agronomic practice? How should an ERA capture system-level selection pressure?

## Case 7 — Golden Rice

- **Background:** Rice engineered to accumulate β-carotene (provitamin A) to address vitamin-A deficiency; several generations (GR1, GR2 with phytoene synthase from maize and bacterial CRTI).
- **Assessment focus:** Compositional equivalence (carotenoid levels intended to differ), allergenicity/toxicity of new proteins (well-characterised enzymes), agronomic performance (early generations underperformed; later events fixed), and gene flow to non-GM rice (context-dependent).
- **Status nuance:** Approved for food use in some jurisdictions (e.g., Philippines 2021) while cultivation decisions and adoption timelines have remained contested and slow — demonstrating that safety assessment and societal uptake are separate gates.
- **Discussion:** How should an ERA treat a trait whose benefit is nutritional (public-health logic) rather than agronomic? Whose risk perception dominates the timeline?

## Case 8 — Virus-resistant papaya (Hawaiian Rainbow)

- **Background:** Papaya ringspot virus devastated Hawaiian production; a coat-protein-mediated resistance strategy saved the industry in the late 1990s.
- **Assessment focus:** Plant-pest considerations (viral sequences), food safety of the coat protein, gene flow (papaya outcrossing is limited), and non-target exposure (minimal — protein is plant-internal).
- **Outcome:** Widely cited success case; also a case in grower adoption economics (non-GM and GM papayas coexist in the same landscape).
- **Discussion:** Why is coat-protein resistance considered low-hazard environmentally? What baseline did the assessment use?

## Case 9 — Genetically modified microorganisms: ice-nucleation bacteria

- **Background:** *Pseudomonas syringae* strains lacking the ice-nucleation protein ("ice-minus") were among the first environmental GMO releases (1980s field tests in the US).
- **Assessment focus:** Survival and dispersal of the bacterium, horizontal gene transfer potential, ecological competition with wild strains, reversibility.
- **Lessons:** Early public opposition and litigation shaped US field-test regulation; established that microorganism releases require survival/dispersal data and containment plans that higher-plant ERAs rarely need.
- **Discussion:** Which ERA elements differ most between plants and microorganisms? Why is reversibility central for GMMs?

## Case 10 — GM salmon (fast-growing AquAdvantage) — environmental containment logic

- **Background:** Atlantic salmon with a growth-hormone regulation construct; approved in the US (2015, after long review) under **containment** conditions — land-locked, multi-barrier facilities, all-female triploid stocks for reproductive containment.
- **Assessment focus:** Escapement risk, establishment in receiving ecosystems, trophic effects, reproductive compatibility with wild conspecifics — addressed primarily through **engineering the exposure to near zero** rather than arguing the hazard is trivial.
- **Lesson:** When hazard reduction is hard, exposure elimination (containment) is a legitimate risk-management strategy.
- **Discussion:** Compare containment-based management with refuge-based management: which evidence does each require?

## Case 11 — Gene-edited crops and divergent regulation

- **Background:** CRISPR-derived crops (e.g., non-browning mushroom/susceptibility-edited crops, waxy corn, herbicide-tolerance edits) illustrate regulatory divergence: some jurisdictions treat small edits like conventional mutants; others (EU currently) regulate them as GMOs pending new-legislation reviews.
- **Assessment focus:** Off-target characterization, unintended-effect screening, comparability to natural variation.
- **Discussion:** If an edit is indistinguishable from a spontaneous mutation, what — if anything — justifies differential assessment? Argue both sides using the hazard/exposure framework.

## Case 12 — Stacked-trait maize (multiple Bt toxins + HT)

- **Background:** Stacks combine several insecticidal proteins and herbicide tolerances to broaden pest spectrum and add management flexibility.
- **Assessment focus:** Each protein assessed individually **plus** interactions; refuges sized to the most demanding component; herbicide-use profile of the whole stack.
- **Discussion:** Is the risk of a stack the sum, the max, or something else? When could protein–protein interactions matter biologically?

## Case 13 — Post-release monitoring detecting change: pink bollworm eradication (India/US)

- **Background:** Bt cotton plus mass pheromone-trap monitoring and sterile-insect releases enabled region-wide pink bollworm eradication programmes — monitoring used as an active management tool, not just compliance paperwork.
- **Lesson:** Monitoring designed with pre-agreed trigger thresholds can enable bold management responses; resistance monitoring for pink bollworm in the US was central to sustaining Bt cotton there.
- **Discussion:** Which monitoring data justified declaring eradication? What false-positive/false-negative trade-offs apply?

## Case 14 — Bt brinjal (eggplant) — assessment vs politics

- **Background:** Bt brinjal (Cry1Ac) developed for fruit-and-shoot-borer control; Bangladesh approved cultivation (2013) after India imposed a moratorium (2010) despite its regulatory committee's assessment finding no convincing food-safety grounds for rejection.
- **Assessment focus:** Food safety of Cry1Ac (long history), gene flow to wild relatives (wild *Solanum* present in South Asia), farmer spraying reduction.
- **Lesson:** A clear example where assessment outputs and regulatory decisions diverged across jurisdictions — ideal for discussing the science/policy boundary.
- **Discussion:** What additional data (if any) would you require before cultivation approval? Who should decide when assessment is complete?

## Case 15 — Environmental monitoring after release: UK Farm-Scale Evaluations

- **Background:** Large multi-year field trials (1999–2003) compared weed and invertebrate communities in HT beet/maize/oilseed-rape systems vs conventional counterparts.
- **Finding:** Effects differed by system — e.g., HT beet and spring rape showed fewer weeds/seed resources (relevant to farmland birds), while HT maize showed more — because herbicide *regimes* differed, not because the trait alone had intrinsic effects.
- **Lesson:** Ecologically meaningful assessment must measure the **management system** at field scale; trait-only thinking misses the real exposure pathways.
- **Discussion:** Why did the maize result differ from the beet result? What would you measure in a Pakistani FSE-style study?

## Common misconceptions

- *"Case outcomes are uniform — GM = safe or GM = risky."* The fifteen cases land on genuinely different risk profiles because traits, organisms, environments, and management differ.
- *"Controversy means bad science somewhere."* Often it means policy values differ (Cases 7, 11, 14).
- *"Monitoring is bureaucratic."* Cases 5 and 13 show monitoring is either the safeguard or the enabler.

## Exam points

- Be able to fill the 12-section template for any case from memory.
- Monarch case: articulate the hazard→exposure→risk chain that resolved it.
- StarLink: explain approval-for-feed-only as an exposure-management device.
- UK FSE: explain why herbicide regime, not trait alone, drove community effects.
- Distinguish assessment divergence (brinjal) from scientific disagreement.

## Quick-check questions

1. Which case best demonstrates that exposure, not hazard, dominated the final risk estimate?
2. Why does Bt cotton's non-target profile improve when compared against sprayed conventional cotton rather than unsprayed cotton?
3. What made StarLink's approval structure fragile?
4. Which case shows containment substituting for hazard characterization?
5. In UK FSE, what was the actual treatment comparison?

### Self-check answers

1. Case 2 (monarch); exposure quantification moved the estimate to negligible.
2. The relevant exposure counterfactual is the insecticide regime being replaced; unsprayed cotton is not the realistic alternative.
3. Feed-only approval relied on commingling controls that failed; no robust traceability/segregation.
4. Case 10 (salmon) — containment reduced exposure to effectively zero.
5. The whole herbicide-management system (HT vs conventional regimes) at field scale, not the transgene per se.
